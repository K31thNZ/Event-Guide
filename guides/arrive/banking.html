Banking & money in Moscow

Western cards don't work in Russia. Here's the complete picture — what you need, how to get it, and how to keep your money moving in both directions.

Updated June 2026 10 min read Not financial advice
Jump to: The reality Opening an account MIR card International transfers Before you arrive
Read this before you move: Banking is the most disruptive practical challenge for Western expats in Moscow. Planning ahead makes an enormous difference. If you have a specific situation — salary in a foreign currency, property overseas, dependants abroad — contact us before you arrive.

The reality in 2026

Since March 2022, Visa and Mastercard suspended all operations in Russia. Cards issued by any bank outside Russia — British, American, European, Australian, Canadian — do not work anywhere in Russia. Not at shops, ATMs, taxis, restaurants, supermarkets, or online Russian services. This is not a temporary inconvenience. It is the permanent baseline for expat financial life in Moscow.

✓ Works in Russia
  • Russian MIR card (domestic payments)
  • Cash rubles (widely accepted everywhere)
  • Russian bank account (Sberbank, Alfa-Bank, T-Bank)
  • Yandex Pay linked to a Russian account
  • SBP fast payment transfers between Russian banks
  • Cash USD/non-EU currency (for exchange)
✗ Does not work
  • Any Visa/Mastercard from a non-Russian bank
  • PayPal, Revolut, Wise, N26, Monzo
  • Apple Pay / Google Pay (on foreign cards)
  • Euro banknotes from export (EU law prohibits)
  • Direct SWIFT from most Western banks
  • Stripe, Square, most Western payment processors

Opening a Russian bank account as a foreigner

Opening a Russian bank account is possible and, for any expat staying longer than a few weeks, essential. The process requires visiting a bank branch in person. Most major banks can open accounts for foreign nationals with the following documents:

  • Passport — original, with a valid Russian visa
  • Migration card — from your border entry
  • Registration document — proof of your Russian address registration
  • Russian phone number — required for account verification (get a SIM first)
  • TIN (INN) — Russian tax identification number (some banks require this; it can be obtained from the tax authority)

Which bank to choose

Sberbank (Сбербанк)

Russia's largest bank. Most branches, most ATMs, most widely accepted. App is in Russian but functional once set up. Some English-speaking staff at larger branches. Generally foreigner-friendly.

Recommended for most expats

Alfa-Bank (Альфа-Банк)

More modern digital banking experience. English-language app available. Historically more accessible for foreigners. Competitive for everyday use and online payments.

Good digital experience

T-Bank (Т-Банк, formerly Tinkoff)

Fully digital, no branches. Excellent app, English-language support available. Account setup is app-based. Requires a courier to deliver your card to a Russian address. Popular with younger expats.

Best app experience

VTB (ВТБ)

Second-largest state bank. Wide ATM network. Process for foreigners can be slower. Good for those employed by large Russian companies who may have corporate relationships.

Corporate-leaning
Practical order of operations: Get your SIM card → complete your address registration → then go to the bank. You need all three in place before the bank will open an account. Plan for this to take 2–3 days after arrival.

MIR card — your essential payment tool

Once your Russian bank account is open, you will be issued a MIR (МИР) card. This is Russia's domestic payment card network, launched in 2015 and now the dominant payment method within Russia. Your MIR card works everywhere in Russia — shops, restaurants, taxis, online Russian services, and contactless payments.

MIR is accepted in a small number of countries outside Russia (primarily some Central Asian, Middle Eastern, and South Asian countries) but is not accepted in any EU, UK, US, Canadian, or Australian bank, shop, or ATM. It cannot be used to shop on Western websites.

Receiving money from abroad

This is where planning ahead matters most. Direct SWIFT transfers from most Western banks to Russian banks are blocked under sanctions. There are legitimate, legal pathways that expats use — but they require setup before and after arrival.

Third-country banking

The most widely used approach: maintain a bank account in a third country that has not imposed sanctions on Russia — Armenia, Kazakhstan, Turkey, UAE, Georgia, or Uzbekistan are the most popular. Receive salary or income into that account, then transfer to Russia via available corridors. This is legal in all these countries and in Russia.

Currency exchange

Bringing physical cash into Russia and exchanging at a bureau de change remains possible. USD is the most useful foreign currency; EU law technically prohibits exporting euro banknotes to Russia. Exchange offices (обмен валюты) are widely available in Moscow city centre and inside metro stations. Rates are competitive.

Banking regulations are one of the fastest-changing areas for expats in Russia. Corridors that work today may be restricted tomorrow, and new options open periodically. For your specific income situation — especially if you're receiving a foreign salary or have overseas property — get in touch with us for up-to-date guidance.

What to prepare before you arrive

  • Open a third-country bank account — Armenian, Kazakh, Turkish, or UAE bank. Do this 4–6 weeks before departure as account opening takes time.
  • Convert money to cash — Bring enough USD cash to cover 4–6 weeks of expenses while your Russian account is being set up.
  • Inform your home bank — Let them know you're moving abroad. Some banks will close accounts of Russian residents; understand your position before you go.
  • Download banking apps — Some Russian bank apps are harder to download once inside Russia (app stores have restrictions). Download alternatives before departure.
  • Understand your tax position — Moving to Russia may affect your tax residency at home. Take advice from a tax professional familiar with your home country and Russia before you go.
