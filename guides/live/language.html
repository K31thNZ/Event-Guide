Learning Russian — a practical expat guide

Cyrillic in a week, survival phrases, the best apps and tutors, and how much Russian you actually need to function in Moscow.

Updated June 2026 10 min read
Jump to: Cyrillic firstSurvival phrasesAppsSchoolsHow much do you need?

Russian is a genuinely difficult language for English speakers — different alphabet, complex grammar, six grammatical cases. But you can get functionally useful much faster than you might think, and living in Moscow gives you unbeatable immersion practice. This guide is realistic: about what you need to survive, what actually works, and what the experience of learning Russian in Moscow is really like.

The good news: Moscow is more navigable without Russian than you'd expect. Metro signs, Google Maps, Yandex, and most major restaurants now have English options. You won't be helpless on day one. But every bit of Russian you learn dramatically improves your quality of life — and Muscovites respond warmly to foreigners who make the effort.

Step one: learn Cyrillic in a week

This is non-negotiable and entirely achievable. The Cyrillic alphabet has 33 letters, many of which are identical to or derived from Latin equivalents. With focused effort, most English speakers can read Cyrillic (not understand it — just read the sounds of letters) within 5–7 days. This alone transforms daily life: you can read street signs, metro stations, menus, shop fronts, and packaging.

The fastest approach is a dedicated 30-minute daily session using a Cyrillic alphabet app or worksheet, followed by active practice — reading every sign you walk past. Apps like "Russian Alphabet — Cyrillic" or the Cyrillic module in Duolingo work well as starters. Aim to know all 33 letters before you arrive or in your first week.

The trap: Several Cyrillic letters look like Latin letters but sound completely different. В sounds like "V", Н sounds like "N", Р sounds like "R", С sounds like "S", Х sounds like "H". Memorise the false friends first to avoid a confusing week of reading signs wrong.

Apps that work

Duolingo Russian

Best for absolute beginners building daily habits. Gamified, free, and covers Cyrillic from the start. Not sufficient on its own for serious progress but excellent for consistent daily practice of 10–15 minutes. Use alongside a tutor or structured course.

Best beginner habit-builder

Anki (with Russian decks)

Spaced repetition flashcards — the most evidence-backed method for vocabulary retention. Download a pre-made Russian core vocabulary deck. 20 minutes a day builds vocabulary significantly faster than most apps. Free on Android; one-time fee on iOS.

Best vocabulary tool

Preply / iTalki

Online marketplaces connecting learners with tutors for 1-on-1 video lessons. Community tutors start from $5–10/hour; qualified teachers from $15–30. Weekly conversational sessions with a native speaker accelerate progress dramatically beyond solo app learning.

Best for speaking confidence

Google Translate camera mode

Not a learning tool, but an essential daily survival tool. Point your phone camera at any Russian text — menus, signs, packaging, forms — and get an instant translation overlaid on the image. Handles Cyrillic well. Available offline with the Russian pack downloaded.

Daily survival tool

Language schools in Moscow

Intensive group courses at Moscow language schools are a popular option for newly arrived expats. They combine structured grammar instruction with immersion — you're learning Russian in Russian at every step. Most schools offer A1/A2 beginner courses specifically designed for English speakers, with morning or evening schedules to fit working expats.

Well-regarded schools include the Pushkin State Russian Language Institute, which offers intensive programmes; the Moscow State University Russian Language courses; and various private schools including Liden & Denz (which runs operations in several Russian cities) and a number of smaller Moscow-based studios. Expat community recommendations via our Connect section tend to be the most reliable guide to current quality and availability.

Research from French-speaking expat communities in Moscow found that over 70% of newcomers who completed an intensive language programme found stable employment matching their qualifications within 12 months — compared to 30% of those with limited Russian.

How much Russian do you actually need day-to-day?

The honest answer: less than you think for basic survival, significantly more than you'd expect for professional and social integration.

For daily life — shopping, eating out, taxis, metro — you can get by with Cyrillic reading + 50 key phrases + Google Translate camera. Most central Moscow restaurants have English menus or picture menus. Yandex Taxi and food apps are in English. Metro signs are bilingual.

For professional integration, healthcare (outside the top expat clinics), dealing with bureaucracy, building genuine friendships with Russian colleagues, and enjoying theatre and cultural life — you need real Russian. Aim for B1 (intermediate) within a year for a good quality of life experience.

One consistent piece of advice from long-term Moscow expats: language exchange (talking to Russians who want to practise English while you practise Russian) is underutilised and extremely effective. The expat community can connect you with Russians looking for language partners — get in touch.
