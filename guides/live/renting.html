Renting a flat in Moscow as a foreigner

The platforms, the process, the registration obligation, and why finding a foreigner-friendly landlord is the most important step.

Updated June 2026 10 min read
Jump to: Where to searchStep-by-step processCostsFurnished vs unfurnishedCommon pitfalls

Finding a flat in Moscow as a foreigner is manageable — but it has specific challenges that differ from the Russian experience. Landlords face extra bureaucratic obligations when renting to foreigners, which means fewer will do it willingly. The platforms are mostly Russian-language. And you need to sort registration simultaneously. Here's the complete playbook.

Prioritise registration: Your landlord must legally register you at the property. This is non-negotiable — you need it for banking, SIM cards, and work permits. Find a landlord willing to do this before signing anything.

Where to search

Three platforms dominate the Moscow rental market:

CIAN (cian.ru)

Russia's largest property platform. The most comprehensive database of Moscow rentals, from private landlords and agencies. Listings include photos, floor plans, and metro proximity filters. Interface is Russian-language — use Google Translate or Chrome's auto-translate. Most expats start and end their search here.

Start here

Avito (avito.ru)

Russia's general classifieds site, with a large rentals section. More private landlords, fewer agencies. Prices sometimes lower than CIAN. Also Russian-language. Good for finding landlords who may be more flexible, though listings quality varies more than on CIAN.

Private landlords

Yandex.Realty (realty.yandex.ru)

Yandex's property platform, increasingly popular and with a slightly cleaner interface than CIAN. Good for cross-referencing listings found elsewhere. Some English-language features available.

Cross-reference

Expat-specific agents

Services like moscow-rentals.ru and moscowestates.com specialise in finding foreigner-friendly landlords. They typically charge one month's rent as commission but do the hard work: making 20–50 phone calls to find landlords willing to register foreigners, arranging viewings, and handling negotiations in Russian.

Worth the commission
The foreigner problem: Most landlord agents on CIAN do not speak English. More importantly, renting to a foreigner creates additional legal obligations — landlords must register you and re-register you every time you re-enter Russia. Finding a landlord willing to do this typically requires 20–50 calls from an agent. Going it alone is possible but significantly harder, especially on a lower budget.

The rental process step by step

  1. Arrange temporary accommodation first. Book a hotel or serviced apartment for your first 1–2 weeks. This gives you a registered address while you flat-hunt, and handles your 7-day registration obligation automatically.
  2. Search and view. Use CIAN filtered by neighbourhood and metro proximity. View 5–10 flats in person — photos frequently don't match reality. Check mobile signal, natural light, noise levels, and the state of the building entrance (подъезд).
  3. Confirm registration willingness before negotiating anything else. Ask directly: "Will you register me?" (Вы можете меня зарегистрировать?) A landlord who won't register you is a landlord you cannot rent from, regardless of how good the flat looks.
  4. Negotiate. In 2026, the rental market has more supply than in 2022–23. Negotiating 10–15% off the listed price is normal, especially on longer leases or winter signings. Ask for the commission to be waived or split.
  5. Sign the lease (договор аренды). Standard leases run 11 months (a 12-month lease triggers different legal requirements). The lease should specify rent, deposit, utilities responsibility, and the registration obligation. Get it translated if your Russian is limited.
  6. Pay deposit and first month. Standard is 1–2 months' deposit plus first month's rent up front. Pay in rubles. Get a written receipt for every payment.
  7. Complete registration. Within 7 days of moving in, your landlord must register you at the address with the MVD. This can be done at an MFC (Multifunctional Centre) — the landlord attends in person with you, with their property documents and your passport.

Furnished vs unfurnished

The vast majority of Moscow rentals are furnished — most listings at mid-range price points include a bed, wardrobe, sofa, kitchen appliances, and often a washing machine. "Fully furnished" (полностью меблированная) means kitchen equipment and white goods too. "With furniture" (с мебелью) means the basics but check what's included. Unfurnished flats (без мебели) are rare at expat price points and mainly targeted at buyers who are also furnishing.

Check the appliances in person — descriptions frequently omit broken or missing items. Run taps, check the oven, test the washing machine, and look inside the fridge during your viewing.

Common pitfalls for expat renters

  • Landlord refuses to register you — this happens. Walk away. You need registration for banking, SIM cards, and work permits.
  • Signing without a Russian-language lease — an English translation isn't legally binding. Have a bilingual contract or a trusted translator review the Russian original.
  • Paying cash without a receipt — always get written proof of every payment. Take photos of meter readings on move-in day.
  • Underestimating utilities — clarify exactly which utilities are included. Gas and electricity bills in winter in older buildings can be substantial.
  • Being re-registered on return trips — every time you leave and re-enter Russia, your landlord must re-register you within 7 days. Confirm they understand and agree to this ongoing obligation.
